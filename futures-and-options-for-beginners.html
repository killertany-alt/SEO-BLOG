Futures and options, often called F&O, are contracts whose value depends on an underlying share or index. They let traders bet on price moves using a smaller amount of money, which is why they are popular and also why they are risky. This guide explains futures and options for beginners, including call and put options, lot size and expiry, in simple language.

What are futures and options?

Futures and options are derivatives, which means their price is derived from another asset such as a share or an index like the Nifty 50. You do not own the share. You own a contract about its future price.

Before using derivatives, you should be comfortable with ordinary share trading. Start with how the stock market works and opening a demat account.

What is a futures contract?

A futures contract is an agreement to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price on a fixed date in the future. Both sides are obliged to complete the deal. If you buy a futures contract and the price rises, you gain. If it falls, you lose.

You only deposit a part of the contract value as margin, so futures are leveraged. A small price move creates a large profit or loss compared with your margin.

What are call and put options?

An option gives you the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell at a fixed price (the strike price) on or before an expiry date. You pay a fee called the premium for this right.

  • A call option gains when the price rises above the strike. Buyers use it when they expect a rise.
  • A put option gains when the price falls below the strike. Buyers use it when they expect a fall.
  • If the price does not move as expected, the option can expire worthless and you lose the premium.

What are lot size and expiry?

You cannot buy a single share in F&O. Contracts trade in fixed units called lots, and the lot size is set by the exchange and revised from time to time. For example, if a share costs ₹1,000 and the lot size is 500, one lot controls a position worth ₹5,00,000.

Every contract also has an expiry date, after which it stops existing. Depending on the contract, expiries can be weekly or monthly. As expiry gets closer, an option loses time value, which is called time decay.

What is the maximum loss for buyers and sellers?

Option buyers can lose only the premium they paid. Option sellers, also called writers, receive the premium but can face very large losses if the price moves sharply against them. Futures buyers and sellers can both lose much more than their margin.

This difference is why sellers need large margins and experience. Position sizing is crucial, so read risk management in stock trading before you try.

Why do most F&O traders lose money?

SEBI's own studies have found that the majority of individual F&O traders lose money over time. Leverage magnifies mistakes, time decay works against option buyers, and costs such as brokerage and taxes are paid even on losing trades.

Emotional decisions make it worse. Many beginners are drawn by small premiums and quick profits, without understanding that a cheap option usually has a low chance of working.

What is margin and why does it matter?

Margin is the money your broker blocks to let you take a futures position or sell an option. It is a fraction of the total contract value, which is what makes leverage possible. If the market moves against you, the broker may ask you to add more margin or close the position.

A small margin does not mean a small risk. Always calculate the full contract value and what a 2% to 5% price move would cost you.

What is time decay?

Time decay is the daily loss of value in an option as it gets closer to expiry. An option has two parts: the real value (how far the price is beyond the strike) and the time value (the chance that it could still move). The time part shrinks every day and is gone at expiry.

This is why an option buyer can be right about the direction but still lose money if the move comes too late.

How should a beginner approach F&O safely?

The safest approach is to learn first and trade later. Follow these steps:

  • Learn the chart basics from technical analysis basics.
  • Paper trade for a few months with no real money.
  • Start by buying options with a small amount you can afford to lose entirely.
  • Use derivatives to hedge a portfolio rather than to gamble.
  • Never borrow money to trade.

F&O is not suitable for everyone. This article is for education and is not investment advice.
