Technical Analysis Basics: Moving Averages, RSI, MACD

Technical analysis is the study of price and volume charts to find patterns that help you time a buy or a sell. It does not look at company profit. It focuses on what buyers and sellers are doing right now. This guide explains technical analysis basics, including the moving average, the RSI indicator and MACD, in simple steps.

What is technical analysis?

Technical analysis is a way of forecasting likely price moves using past price and volume. Its core idea is that all known information is already in the price and that human behaviour repeats, so similar patterns appear again and again.

It is popular with traders who buy and sell over days or weeks. Investors who hold for years usually rely more on fundamental analysis. Many people use both: fundamentals to choose the share and technicals to choose the timing.

What are the basic tools of technical analysis?

Start with the three basics before any indicator: the trend, support and resistance, and volume. If you are not comfortable with them, read our guide on how to read stock charts first.

Indicators are calculations drawn on top of the chart. They fall into two groups. Trend indicators, such as moving averages, show direction. Momentum indicators, such as RSI and MACD, show the speed of a move and whether it may be tiring.

What is a moving average and how do you use it?

A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of days, redrawn every day. It smooths out noise and shows the trend. A 20 day average reacts quickly, while a 200 day average shows the long term direction.

A simple use is to watch the price against the line. A price above a rising average suggests an uptrend. When a short average, such as 10 days, crosses above a longer one, such as 30 days, traders call it a bullish crossover. A cross below is bearish.

What is the RSI indicator?

The relative strength index (RSI) is a momentum indicator that moves between 0 and 100. It compares recent gains to recent losses. A reading above 70 is usually called overbought, meaning the price rose fast and may pause. A reading below 30 is called oversold, meaning the price fell fast and may bounce.

RSI is not a sell signal by itself. In a strong uptrend it can stay above 70 for weeks. Use it to spot warning signs and to confirm what the trend and support levels already suggest.

What is MACD?

MACD stands for moving average convergence divergence. It subtracts a slow average from a fast one and plots the result as a line with a signal line and a histogram. When the MACD line crosses above the signal line, momentum is turning up. When it crosses below, momentum is fading.

Because MACD is built from moving averages, it lags the price. It works best in trending markets and can give false signals in sideways ones.

How do you combine indicators without confusion?

Choose one trend tool and one momentum tool and keep the rest of the chart clean. For example, use the 20 and 50 day averages for trend and RSI for momentum. Then wait until at least two signals agree before acting.

  • Trend: is the price above or below its moving averages?
  • Momentum: does RSI or MACD agree with the move?
  • Levels: is there support or resistance nearby?
  • Plan: where is your stop-loss and your target?

What is the difference between SMA and EMA?

A simple moving average (SMA) gives equal weight to every day in the period. An exponential moving average (EMA) gives more weight to recent prices, so it reacts faster to new moves. Short term traders often prefer the EMA, while long term investors often use the SMA.

The 50 day and 200 day averages are widely watched. When the 50 day average crosses above the 200 day, traders call it a golden cross, and when it crosses below, a death cross.

How can you practise technical analysis safely?

Open a chart of any share and scroll back a year. Hide the right side, guess what happens next using your tools and then reveal it. Repeat this on many charts until you see which signals worked.

Then paper trade for a month with a written plan for entry, stop-loss and target. Only after your results are consistent should you use real money, in a small quantity.

What are the limits of technical analysis?

Indicators lag, patterns fail and news can overrule any chart in minutes. Technical analysis improves your odds but never gives certainty, so every trade needs a stop-loss and a small position size. Read our guide on risk management in stock trading before you trade with real money.

Test your ideas by paper trading first, which means placing practice trades without real money. When a method works for several months on paper, you can start small with real capital.
