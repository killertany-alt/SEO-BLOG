How to Read Stock Charts: Candlesticks for Beginners

A stock chart is a picture of how a share's price has moved over time. Learning how to read stock charts helps you see the trend, spot where buyers and sellers have acted before and avoid buying at the wrong moment. This guide covers the candlestick chart, trends, support and resistance and volume in plain words.

What does a stock chart show?

A stock chart shows the price of a share on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal axis. Most charts also show volume, which is the number of shares traded. Together they tell you where the price has been, how fast it moved and how many people took part.

A chart cannot predict the future. It shows what buyers and sellers have done so far, which helps you judge probabilities. It works best together with the company analysis in our guide to fundamental analysis.

What are the main types of stock charts?

The three common types are line charts, bar charts and candlestick charts. A line chart joins closing prices and is the simplest view. Bar and candlestick charts show four prices for each period: open, high, low and close.

Most traders prefer the candlestick chart because it is easy to read at a glance. You can switch the timeframe from one minute to one month. Long term investors usually look at daily and weekly charts, while day traders watch shorter ones.

How do you read a candlestick chart?

Each candle shows what the price did during one period. The thick part is the body, running from the open to the close. The thin lines are wicks, which show the highest and lowest prices reached. A green candle means the price closed higher than it opened, and a red candle means it closed lower.

Long bodies show strong buying or selling, while long wicks show that the price was pushed away from an extreme. A tiny body with wicks on both sides, called a doji, shows indecision between buyers and sellers.

What is a trend in a stock chart?

A trend is the general direction of the price. In an uptrend the price makes higher highs and higher lows. In a downtrend it makes lower highs and lower lows. When it moves within a range without direction, it is a sideways trend.

Identify the trend first. A simple rule is to look for buying chances in uptrends and to be careful in downtrends. You can confirm a trend with moving averages, which we explain in our guide to technical analysis basics.

What are support and resistance?

Support is a price level where a falling share tends to stop and bounce because buyers step in. Resistance is a level where a rising share tends to stall because sellers step in. These levels form because many traders remember the same prices.

When price breaks above resistance, that old ceiling often becomes new support. When it breaks below support, the old floor often becomes resistance. Draw these levels as horizontal lines across the most obvious highs and lows.

Why does volume matter on a chart?

Volume shows how many shares were traded in a period, and it tells you how much conviction is behind a move. A price breakout on high volume is more trustworthy than one on low volume, because more participants agreed with the move.

A price rising on falling volume can be a warning that buyers are losing interest. Volume bars sit at the bottom of the chart and are usually coloured green or red to match the candles.

What are common candlestick patterns?

A few patterns are worth knowing. A hammer has a small body and a long lower wick and suggests buyers rejected lower prices. A shooting star is the opposite, with a long upper wick that suggests sellers rejected higher prices. An engulfing candle is a large candle that fully covers the previous one and shows a shift in control.

No pattern works on its own. Treat it as a clue, then confirm it with the trend, support and resistance and volume.

Which timeframe should beginners use?

Beginners should start with the daily chart, where each candle is one trading day. It filters out the noise of minute by minute swings. Check the weekly chart to see the bigger trend and use a shorter timeframe only to fine tune an entry.

A good habit is to look at three timeframes: weekly for the big picture, daily for the plan and a shorter one for timing. If they all agree, the signal is stronger.

What mistakes should beginners avoid when reading charts?

The most common mistakes are listed below:

  • Adding too many indicators, which makes the chart confusing and the signals contradictory.
  • Ignoring the bigger timeframe and trading only on a one minute chart.
  • Seeing perfect patterns everywhere because you want a trade.
  • Entering without a stop-loss, which we cover in our guide to risk management in stock trading.

Practise on past charts first, then on a small amount of real money. Chart reading is a skill that improves with repetition.
